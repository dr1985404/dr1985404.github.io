Mastering Canadian Medical Ethics for MCCQE

Essential medico-legal principles for International Medical Graduates preparing for Canadian licensure.

The Four Pillars of Bioethics in Canada

The Canadian medical system is built upon four fundamental ethical principles that guide clinical decision-making. For the MCCQE, understanding the practical application of these principles is more important than knowing their definitions.

  • Autonomy: The right of a capable patient to make their own healthcare decisions, including the right to refuse life-saving treatment.
  • Beneficence: The duty of the physician to act in the best interest of the patient.
  • Non-maleficence: The duty to "do no harm" and avoid unnecessary interventions.
  • Justice: The fair and equitable distribution of healthcare resources.
Clinical Scenario: A capable 70-year-old patient refuses a blood transfusion due to personal beliefs. Despite the physician's duty of beneficence, the principle of Autonomy prevails, and the refusal must be respected.

Informed Consent and Capacity Assessment

In Canada, consent is a process, not just a signature. For consent to be valid, it must be voluntary, informed, and given by a person with the legal capacity to do so. Physicians are responsible for assessing capacity clinically. A patient is considered capable if they can understand the information relevant to the decision and appreciate the reasonably foreseeable consequences of a decision or lack thereof.

Confidentiality and Mandatory Reporting

While confidentiality is a cornerstone of the patient-physician relationship, there are specific legal requirements in Canada where it must be breached. These include:

  • Child Abuse: Suspected physical, sexual, or emotional abuse of a minor must be reported to child protective services immediately.
  • Public Safety: Reporting medically unfit drivers to provincial authorities.
  • Communicable Diseases: Reporting specific "notifiable" diseases to public health units.
  • Gunshot Wounds: Reporting to the police (requirements vary by province).

Medical Assistance in Dying (MAiD)

Since its legalization, MAiD has become a high-yield topic for the MCCQE. Candidates must understand the eligibility criteria: the patient must be an adult, eligible for Canadian health services, have a grievous and irremediable medical condition, and provide informed consent at multiple stages of the process.
